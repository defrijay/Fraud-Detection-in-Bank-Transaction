## 📌 Executive Summary

Banks lose money and customer trust every time a fraudulent transaction slips through — and the problem is getting harder as digital transaction volume grows and fraud tactics evolve faster than static, rule-based defenses can keep up.

The catch: most of a bank's historical transaction data has **no fraud label**. Nobody has manually confirmed which transactions were actually fraudulent, so building a traditional supervised model is not straightforward — you can't train on ground truth you don't have.

This project shows a practical way around that constraint:

1. **Discover** likely fraud patterns directly from raw, unlabeled transactions using clustering and anomaly detection (K-Means, Isolation Forest).
2. **Learn** from those discovered patterns using supervised models (Random Forest, Logistic Regression, ANN, DNN) that can generalize to new, unseen transactions.
3. **Compare** models on validation and test data to identify which approach is most reliable.

**Headline result:** the simplest models — Random Forest and Logistic Regression — matched or outperformed the deep learning models on this dataset, reaching ~99% test accuracy, while also being cheaper to build, faster to run, and easier to explain to auditors and regulators.

**The honest caveat:** because the "fraud" label itself was generated by clustering rather than confirmed by human investigators, these results describe how well the models learned the *patterns the clustering step found* — not necessarily how well they'd catch real-world fraud. See [Limitations & What's Next](#-limitations--whats-next) for what would need to happen before this goes into production.

## The Business Challenge

### Background

Banks are being squeezed from two directions at once: digital transaction volume (mobile banking, ATM, online) keeps climbing, while fraud tactics keep getting more sophisticated and adaptive. Every fraudulent transaction that slips through is not just a direct financial loss — it also erodes customer trust, damages reputation, and can trigger regulatory scrutiny (for example, obligations to report suspicious transactions to financial authorities).

The challenge is structural, not just technical: banks sit on huge volumes of transaction data, but **most of it has no confirmed fraud label**. Manually investigating and labeling every suspicious transaction is slow, expensive, and doesn't scale. Meanwhile, conventional rule-based systems (e.g., "flag if amount > $X" or "flag if location differs from usual") are easy for sophisticated fraudsters to learn and route around.

From a business standpoint, what's needed is a system that can:

- **Learn from the transaction patterns themselves** rather than relying only on static rules — so it can catch fraud modes nobody has seen before.
- **Turn unlabeled data into a usable asset** for training predictive models, without waiting on expensive manual labeling.
- **Balance strict detection against customer experience** — flagging too many legitimate transactions (false positives) frustrates customers and increases support costs, while missed fraud (false negatives) hits the bottom line directly.

This project addresses that with a **hybrid unsupervised → supervised approach**: clustering (K-Means, Isolation Forest) surfaces anomalous patterns in unlabeled data, and those patterns are then used to train predictive models (Random Forest, Logistic Regression, ANN, DNN) that can be evaluated, compared, and — eventually — operationalized for early detection.

**Key Business Aspects:**
- **Sector:** Banking and Financial Services
- **Business focus:** Minimize fraud losses while protecting customer experience
- **Data condition:** Real bank transaction data, with no explicit fraud label
- **Strategic approach:** Unsupervised learning as a bridge to a scalable, adaptive predictive detection system

### Problem Statement

The bank currently has **no effective, real-time fraud detection system**. Any existing system likely relies on rule-based logic that sophisticated fraudsters can learn to evade. Without labeled data, the bank struggles to:

1. Accurately **distinguish normal transactions from fraudulent ones**
2. **Identify emerging fraud patterns** as they evolve
3. **Act proactively** before losses occur, rather than reactively after the fact
4. **Allocate investigation resources efficiently** instead of chasing every alert manually

**Business consequences of inaction:**
- Potential financial losses reaching into the billions (in local currency terms)
- Declining customer trust and reputational damage
- Regulatory penalty risk and compliance exposure
- Wasted analyst hours on inefficient manual investigation

## Business Questions We Set Out to Answer

Each question below is framed to connect directly to a business decision — not just a model metric.

#### 1. Can pattern-based detection actually find fraud in data with no labels?
- How effective is clustering (K-Means, Isolation Forest) at separating normal transactions from potentially fraudulent ones, given that there's no ground-truth fraud label to validate against?
- What transaction patterns (amount, timing, frequency, login behavior) consistently form the "high-risk" cluster — and do those patterns make business sense, rather than just being a statistical artifact?

#### 2. Which model actually supports good operational decisions?
- Among the models tested (Random Forest, Logistic Regression, ANN, DNN), which offers the best balance between **catching as much fraud as possible (recall)** and **minimizing disruption to legitimate customers (precision / false positives)**?
- Does the added complexity of deep learning (ANN/DNN) deliver enough accuracy gain to justify its implementation and maintenance cost, compared to simpler, easier-to-audit models like Random Forest or Logistic Regression?

#### 3. What fraud indicators can the risk team actually act on?
- Which transaction behaviors (e.g., number of login attempts, transaction timing, balance-to-amount ratio) most strongly signal potential fraud — and how can these findings be translated into concrete rules or alerts for the fraud investigation team?
- Is fraud risk driven more by transaction-level characteristics (amount, timing) or account-level behavior (frequency, channel habits) — and what does that imply for designing different mitigation strategies for each?

#### 4. Is this feasible to actually run in production?
- How could this detection system be integrated into the bank's real-time transaction flow without adding latency that hurts the customer experience?
- Which evaluation metric (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, or some combination) should actually drive business decisions here — given that a missed fraud case (false negative) and a wrongly blocked legitimate customer (false positive) carry very different financial and reputational costs?

## Our Approach

This project tackles fraud detection in **unlabeled** bank transaction data using an unsupervised → supervised hybrid pipeline:

1. **Unsupervised Learning** — use clustering algorithms (K-Means, Isolation Forest) to surface anomalous transactions and generate a working "fraud" label
2. **Supervised Learning** — train ML and DL models on those generated labels
3. **Evaluation** — compare model performance with proper train/validation/test discipline

### Highlights
- ✅ Unsupervised label generation via K-Means and Isolation Forest
- ✅ 2 Machine Learning models: Random Forest, Logistic Regression

- ✅ 5-fold cross-validation for ML models
- ✅ Full visualization suite: EDA, clustering results, training curves, confusion matrices
- ✅ Feature importance analysis to surface key fraud indicators

## How It Works

### 1. Data Preprocessing
- Median imputation for missing numerical values
- Timestamp parsing for date fields
- Validation checks for duplicates and outliers

### 2. Feature Engineering

**Temporal features**
- `Transaction_Hour`, `Transaction_Day`, `Transaction_Month`, `Transaction_DayOfWeek`

**Behavioral features**
- `Time_Since_Last_Transaction` — hours since the account's previous transaction
- `Transaction_Frequency` — number of transactions per account
- `Balance_to_Amount_Ratio` — post-transaction balance relative to transaction size

**Encoded features**
- Label encoding for categorical fields (`TransactionType`, `Location`, `Channel`, `CustomerOccupation`)

### 3. Turning Unlabeled Data into Labels

**K-Means Clustering**
```python
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=2, random_state=42)
labels = kmeans.fit_predict(X_scaled)
```
- Splits the data into 2 clusters (assumed normal vs. fraud)
- The smaller cluster is treated as the fraud proxy
- Silhouette score used to sanity-check cluster separation quality

**Isolation Forest**
```python
iso_forest = IsolationForest(contamination=0.1, random_state=42)
anomalies = iso_forest.fit_predict(X_scaled)
```
- Purpose-built anomaly detection algorithm
- Flags ~10% of the data as potential anomalies
- Works on the principle that anomalies are easier to "isolate" than normal points

### 4. Model Training

**Data split:**
- Training: 70%
- Validation: 15%
- Test: 15%
- Stratified sampling to preserve class balance across splits

**What this means for the business:** the two simplest, cheapest models to build and explain — Random Forest and Logistic Regression — matched or beat the deep learning models on test accuracy. That's a meaningful finding: on a dataset of this size and complexity, there's currently no accuracy justification for the extra cost, training time, and reduced interpretability that come with deep learning.

### Top 10 Fraud Indicators (Random Forest)

1. `LoginAttempts` — elevated login attempts are a strong suspicious-behavior signal
2. `TransactionAmount` — unusually large or small amounts
3. `AccountBalance` — balance-level patterns
4. `Balance_to_Amount_Ratio` — balance relative to transaction size
5. `Time_Since_Last_Transaction` — how recently the account was last active
6. `Transaction_Hour` — unusual timing (e.g., late night)
7. `Transaction_Frequency` — overall account activity level
8. `TransactionDuration` — how long the transaction took to process
9. `CustomerAge` — age-related patterns
10. `Channel_Encoded` — channel preference (Online / ATM / Branch)

**Reading these as a story:** the strongest signals cluster around *behavioral* anomalies (login attempts, timing, frequency) rather than purely *demographic* ones (age). That's a useful business signal in itself — it suggests investigation effort is better spent building behavioral monitoring than profiling customers by static attributes.

## 📐 How We Measured Success

### Confusion Matrix, in Plain Terms
- **True Positive:** fraud correctly caught
- **True Negative:** legitimate transaction correctly cleared
- **False Positive:** legitimate transaction wrongly flagged — a customer-experience cost
- **False Negative:** fraud that got through undetected — the most costly error

### The Metrics
```
Accuracy  = (TP + TN) / Total
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Recall    = TP / (TP + FN)
F1-Score  = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
```

### Why not just look at accuracy?
- **Accuracy** tells you overall correctness, but can be misleading when fraud is rare
- **Precision** tells you how many "fraud" alerts are actually real — low precision means annoyed customers and wasted investigator time
- **Recall** tells you how much real fraud you're actually catching — the metric that matters most when a miss is expensive
- **F1-score** balances the two when neither error type clearly dominates

## 💡 Business Impact & Recommendations

1. **Start with the simple models.** Random Forest and Logistic Regression delivered the best accuracy *and* the lowest cost to build, run, and explain to auditors. There's no evidence here that the added complexity of ANN/DNN is worth it yet — that could change with more data or richer features (e.g., network/graph features), but it's not justified today.

2. **Random Forest doubles as an insight engine, not just a classifier.** Its feature importance output is a ready-made starting point for the risk team: login-attempt anomalies, unusual timing, and balance-to-amount ratios are the highest-signal areas to build monitoring rules and investigator playbooks around.

3. **Behavioral signals outweigh static ones.** The strongest fraud indicators describe what an account is *doing* right now, not who the customer *is*. Investment in behavioral tracking (login patterns, transaction timing, frequency) will likely pay off more than demographic profiling.

4. **Treat the reported accuracy as a ceiling, not a guarantee.** The "fraud" label used to train every model here was generated by clustering, not confirmed by a human investigator. Before any of these numbers are used to justify a production rollout, the model's flags need to be validated against real investigated cases.

## ⚠️ Limitations & What's Next

Being transparent about what this project does *not* yet answer is as important as reporting what it found:

- **Cluster validity isn't independently confirmed.** K-Means and Isolation Forest weren't cross-checked against each other, and there's no analysis showing that the "fraud" cluster's characteristics make business sense beyond being smaller in size. **Next step:** compare the two labeling methods for agreement, and profile the average feature values inside vs. outside the flagged cluster.
- **Precision/recall trade-offs weren't turned into a recommendation.** Classification reports were generated per model, but the model comparison focused on accuracy. **Next step:** make the recall-vs-precision trade-off explicit and pick a model (and a decision threshold) based on the actual cost of a false negative vs. a false positive.
- **No real-time feasibility assessment.** Latency, integration into the live transaction pipeline, and infrastructure requirements haven't been evaluated. **Next step:** benchmark inference time per transaction and design the scoring pipeline before assuming any model here is "real-time ready."
- **No production artifacts yet.** Trained models aren't currently serialized (`.pkl` / `.h5`) or served via an API. **Next step:** persist the winning model, wrap it in a lightweight API (e.g., FastAPI), and add monitoring for performance/data drift.
